\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Cortical cultures improve at Pong when misses are followed by unpredictable stimulation, which has been read as support for the free-energy principle~\cite{Kagan2022}. But the post-miss stimulus is also more disruptive: twice the amplitude and followed by eight seconds without input. We show that this alone suffices: if failure perturbs the network more than success, the network stays in each setting in inverse proportion to its failure rate, and improves with no prediction or reward. A closed-loop model reproduces the reported conditions and predicts anti-learning when success is more disruptive. We propose experiments that separate the two accounts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Two differences, one interpretation}

In DishBrain~\cite{Kagan2022}, after a hit the culture receives a ``predictable'' stimulus: 100~Hz for 100~ms on all eight electrodes at the sensory amplitude, 75~mV. After a miss it receives an ``unpredictable'' one: 150~mV at 5~Hz, at random sites and times, for 4~s, then 4~s without stimulation. The authors chose the higher voltage so that it would force action potentials ``regardless of the state the cell was in, thereby being even more disruptive to the culture''~\cite{Kagan2022}. The post-miss feedback is therefore both less predictable and more disruptive, and the published controls do not separate the two: \emph{No feedback} removes both differences, and \emph{Silent} still withdraws the sensory input for eight seconds after a miss only. Earlier criticism concerned the words ``intelligence'' and ``sentience''~\cite{Balci2023,Kagan2023reply}; our question is which property of the feedback teaches.

\section{The scrambler}

Let $\psi$ be the network's setting and $P(\psi)$ its probability of missing. Suppose a miss moves $\psi$ by a symmetric kernel $K(\psi,\psi')=K(\psi',\psi)$ and a hit leaves it in place. Nothing tells the network which way to change.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:exact}
If $P>0$ and $K$ is irreducible, the stationary distribution is $\rho(\psi)\propto1/P(\psi)$. The long-run miss rate is the harmonic mean of $P$, which is below its arithmetic mean (the miss rate of a random setting) unless $P$ is constant.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\rho\propto1/P$ satisfies detailed balance, $\rho(\psi)P(\psi)K(\psi,\psi')=\rho(\psi')P(\psi')K(\psi',\psi)$, because $K$ is symmetric; the rest is the AM--HM inequality.
\end{proof}

A setting that misses ten times less often is kept ten times longer: good settings are not sought, only left alone. In general both outcomes perturb, with variance $\sigma_m^2$ after a miss and $\sigma_h^2$ after a hit. For small isotropic steps the setting diffuses with $D(\psi)=\tfrac12[\sigma_m^2P+\sigma_h^2(1-P)]$, and the It\^o form of the diffusion gives $\rho\propto1/D$~\cite{Schnitzer1993}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kappa}
With $\kappa=\sigma_h^2/\sigma_m^2$, $\rho\propto1/[\kappa+(1-\kappa)P]$. The stationary miss rate is below the uniform average for $\kappa<1$ (learning), equal to it for $\kappa=1$, and above it for $\kappa>1$ (anti-learning).
\end{proposition}

Predictability does not enter; what matters is how far each outcome moves the network. The principle is old: Ashby's ultrastability~\cite{Ashby1952}, orthokinesis~\cite{Schnitzer1993}, the stimulus regulation principle in cultures~\cite{Shahaf2001,Marom2002} and learning by stimulation avoidance~\cite{Sinapayen2017,Masumori2020}. None of these works analyses DishBrain. We add that DishBrain's own feedback contains this contingency, its quantitative consequences including the sign reversal at $\kappa=1$, and experiments that decide.

\section{A closed-loop model}

On a two-parameter paddle model ($31\times21$ grid of settings) Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} predicts a stationary miss rate of $0.815$; simulation gives $0.810$, against $0.845$ for a random setting. In a DishBrain-like loop (\texttt{scrambler.py}) the ball is place- and rate-coded on 8 channels as in the experiment, two motor populations read it through 16 plastic weights, and the only teacher is a random kick to all weights after each approach (s.d.\ $\sigma_m$ after a miss, $\sigma_h$ after a hit). Sessions of 2000 approaches compare the first quarter with the rest, like the first 5 of 20 minutes. Silent is modelled as a weaker scrambler ($\sigma_m=0.03$ against $0.08$), because without stimulation unstimulated cultures burst~\cite{Wagenaar2005} and bursts make weights drift~\cite{Chao2005}; this is a testable assumption.

Over 400 simulated cultures the within-session gain in hit fraction is $+0.161\pm0.014$ for Stimulus, $+0.068\pm0.012$ for Silent and $+0.017\pm0.007$ for No feedback: the reported ordering, with Silent above No feedback but weaker~\cite{Kagan2022}. The same kick after every outcome gives $-0.010\pm0.007$, and reversed disruption $-0.019\pm0.006$. Performance falls monotonically with $\kappa$ and crosses the information-free level at $\kappa=1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}b). The model is not fitted to data; the claim is only that the contingency suffices.

Two analyses of existing recordings also decide. \emph{Per-event displacement:} the scrambler predicts that a miss moves the network's state (e.g.\ the evoked response to a fixed probe) further than a hit, and that across cultures the gain grows with this difference. \emph{Bursts after a miss:} in Silent, the number of spontaneous bursts after misses should predict each culture's gain.

\section{Discussion}

If the scrambler account holds, DishBrain shows a generic property of plastic networks under outcome-contingent perturbation rather than predictive processing, though a surprise-minimizing system may implement exactly this. It also explains why gains did not carry over between sessions~\cite{Kagan2022}: a setting held only by the absence of perturbation is diffused by bursting~\cite{Chao2005}. \emph{Limitations:} stimulation is not isotropic, and a 100~Hz burst may itself potentiate pathways~\cite{Jimbo1999}; the displacement analysis measures this directly.
